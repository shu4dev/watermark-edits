# Text watermark detection under edits (CS578 proposal)
Soleil Pham, Brian Shu, Mary Wanduka, Anastasios Manganaris. Text of docs/proposal.pdf, extracted 2026-10-25; the hyperlink to the GPT response is not in the PDF text.

## Research problem and motivation
Language model watermarking is a mainstream statistical problem concerned with schemes to imperceptibly alter language-model text so that it may be uniquely identified as artificially generated, with robustness to edits applied by resource-constrained actors.

**Setting.** A generator samples y_{1:n} from p_t = p(· | x, y_{<t}) over a vocabulary V. A watermarking scheme W for a generator consists of a key generator, a watermarked generation procedure based on a key, and a detection procedure based on the key. In green-list watermarking, the key is a sequence of independent green lists G_t ⊆ V. Each token is in G_t with probability γ. Watermarked generation samples y_t ~ p̃_t(v) ∝ p_t(v) e^{δ 1[v ∈ G_t]}. The attacker makes at most ρn substitutions, each from a permitted set R(y_t) ⊂ V (e.g., synonyms for y_t), and is key-blind (knows γ, δ, p_t, not the key). After analyzing this case, we will also analyze the case when an attacker can see green lists G_t at each position t independently with probability λ (λ-leakage). The detector knows the key, sees only the edited z_{1:n}, and must have false-positive probability at most α on any key-independent sequence. Edits made to fool the detector are substitutions only.

**Goal.** Characterize the minimax false-negative exponent E(ρ) = liminf_{n→∞} −(1/n) log β*_n(α, ρ), where β*_n(α, ρ) is the minimax false-negative probability at edit fraction ρ and false-positive limit α. After fully characterizing the exponent, obtain the critical edit fraction ρ*, the boundary after which E(ρ > ρ*) = 0.

## The remaining gap
Green-list watermark detectors [2] are framed as hypothesis tests with the objective of minimizing false positives [3]. While prior work models human edits for asymptotic optimality [4], these approaches do not characterize worst-case attacks under fixed budgets or partial key knowledge. In practice, substitution attacks [5] and watermark stealing [6] can remove watermarks by partially learning the key. Despite their empirical success, these attack evaluations lack theoretical characterizations of the minimax false-negative probability E(ρ) and critical edit fraction ρ*. Golowich and Moitra [1] provide a watermark scheme that is undetectable, sound (low false-positive rate), and edit-robust (low false-negative rate for a lower-bounded constant fraction of adversarial edits). However, the upper bound on E(ρ) and the ρ* beyond which the watermark fails is not fully determined against optimal, budget-limited attackers as a function of key knowledge. We aim to address this gap for a simplified, substitution-only setting.

## Proposed approach
To fully characterize the exponent we must obtain and match lower (achievability) and upper (converse) bounds. For the lower bound, choose a detector φ and find the infimum exponent of this detector against every admissible attack (those making up to ρn substitutions): find and prove that a certain attack strategy A* dominates all other admissible attacks, then find the exponent for that detector–attack pair, inf_{A ∈ A_ρ} E(φ, A) = E(φ, A*). For the upper bound, find the exponent of the best possible detector against that optimal attack, sup_{φ'} E(φ', A*). To conclude the bounds match for every ρ, use that A* is the worst attack against φ and show the original φ achieves the optimal false-negative rate against this attack. With matched bounds, derive the critical edit fraction at which E(ρ) = 0.

## Can AI solve this problem
GPT-6 Astra, given a well-specified version of the problem, claimed a "substantial partial solution" under the simplifying assumption that token generation is independent; for the autoregressive version it obtained a lower bound on the exponent but no upper bound (we suspect it rests on [1]). Discussion with Claude Opus 5.5 yielded the strategy above: lower bound from the green-count test in the high-entropy regime as the detector and the priority-rule attack as the dominant attack; upper bound by showing the Neyman–Pearson test is an optimal detector that does not beat that exponent, via Stein's lemma. Both are starting points; the remaining upper bound in the autoregressive case is a non-trivial contribution.
